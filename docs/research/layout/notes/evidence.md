# Evidence base for dashboard layout: perception, attention and UX research on information density and whitespace

Scope note: sources marked **[fetched]** were read in full (or the primary PDF was read); sources marked **[snippet]** were only seen as search-result excerpts and should be treated as less certain. Vendor sources are flagged as such.

## 1. Stephen Few and Tufte: rules for at-a-glance monitoring dashboards, what to put first, KPI with context

### Takeaway
Few's core position is that a monitoring dashboard is a dense but calm single screen: everything needed, nothing decorative, every key number accompanied by context (target + history), and colour reserved for the few items that need attention. "Airy" in Few/Tufte terms means removing non-data ink (borders, gauges, gradients, decoration), not removing data: Few explicitly wants "a dense array of information in a small amount of space".

### Cited Findings
- Few defines a dashboard as a "single-screen display of the most important information needed to do a job, designed for rapid monitoring". **[fetched, primary]** — [Few, "Why Most Dashboards Fail", Perceptual Edge 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few: "dashboards must display a dense array of information in a small amount of space in a manner that communicates clearly and immediately", leveraging "the human brain to sense and process several chunks of information rapidly". **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few: "The fundamental challenge of dashboard design is to display all the required information on a single screen: clearly and without distraction; in a manner that can be quickly examined and understood." **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few's three-step monitoring sequence the layout must support: (1) "getting an overview of what's going on and quickly identifying what needs attention"; (2) looking "more closely at each of those areas that need attention"; (3) if more detail is needed, "the dashboard should serve as a seamless launch pad to that information". **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few on what to include: "You must condense the information, you must include only what you absolutely need... Anything that doesn't add meaning to the data must be thrown out". **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few on KPI context: a bare value "without additional context would tell you little. Compared to what? ... how good or bad? Are we on track? Is this better than before? The right context for the key measures makes the difference between numbers that just sit there on the screen and those that enlighten and inspire action." **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few's worked example is a KPI list with four columns per metric: sparkline ("Past 12 months"), metric name, bullet graph ("% of Target"), and the actual number. It shows seven metrics, "each ... enriched with historical context and compared to performance targets", in roughly the space three gauges took. **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few on bullet graphs: "The prominent horizontal bar is the metric, the small vertical line is a comparative measure (a target in this case), and the varying intensities of gray in the background indicate the qualitative states of poor, satisfactory, and good." He created them "to replace the gauges and meters ... with a richer form of display that requires much less space". **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few on sparklines vs. trend arrows: sparklines "enhance what is often displayed using trend arrows by actually showing changes through time ... They provide historical context for what's happening now." **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few on colour as an alert channel: "Because no colors other than blacks and grays appear anywhere in the display other than the red icon, nothing distracts you from quickly finding what needs your attention most, with nothing more than a glance." **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few's 13 common mistakes (from *Information Dashboard Design*); the six confirmed from a fetched secondary summary are: (1) exceeding the boundaries of a single screen, (2) supplying inadequate context for the data, (3) displaying excessive detail or precision, (4) choosing a deficient measure, (5) choosing inappropriate display media, (6) introducing meaningless variety. **[fetched, secondary]** — [The Data School, "13 Dashboard Design Mistakes (Part 1)"](https://www.thedataschool.co.uk/anh-vu/are-you-making-these-13-dashboard-design-mistakes/)
- Two further mistakes from the list — cluttering the display with useless decoration, and misusing/overusing colour — are reported in Part 2 of the same summary. **[snippet]** — [The Data School, Part 2](https://www.thedataschool.co.uk/anh-vu/are-you-making-these-13-dashboard-design-mistakes-part-2)
- Tufte defines sparklines as "small, intense, simple, word-sized graphic[s] with typographic resolution", with a data-ink ratio of 1.0 (no frames, tick marks or other non-data elements); several can be grouped as a small multiple. **[snippet of primary]** — [Tufte, "Sparkline theory and practice"](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparkline-theory-and-practice-edward-tufte/)
- The data-ink ratio is the proportion of a graphic's ink devoted to the non-redundant display of data-information; Tufte's rule is to maximise it. **[snippet]** — [Tufte's Principles of Data-Ink (course text)](https://jtr13.github.io/cc19/tuftes-principles-of-data-ink.html)
- Small multiples: placing multiple same-format graphs next to each other to allow direct visual comparison. **[snippet]** — [R Journal RJ-2015-003](https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2015-003/RJ-2015-003.pdf)
- Counter-evidence to extreme minimalism: a study reported in the same R Journal article found the majority of evaluators were more attracted to the standard graph than to a Tufte-minimalist version. **[snippet]** — [R Journal RJ-2015-003](https://journal.r-project.org/articles/RJ-2015-003/RJ-2015-003.pdf)
- NN/g (Laubheimer 2017), citing Cleveland & McGill (1985): length and 2D position are the most accurately judged encodings ("we are quite adept at estimating how lengths compare, and we can also accurately estimate position in a 2D space"); area and angle are judged poorly. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, "Dashboards: Making Charts and Graphs Easier to Understand"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)
- NN/g: pie/donut charts "should be avoided most of the time"; gauges "consume a lot of precious space"; treemaps "are not appropriate for simple, actionable dashboards"; 3D "will make the chart much more difficult to interpret quickly and accurately". **[fetched]** — [NN/g dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)
- NN/g: "color should not be used to communicate information about quantitative values or magnitude"; hue/saturation "are helpful as a secondary grouping cue"; colour plus shape is a stronger signal than either alone. **[fetched]** — [NN/g dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)
- Colour-blindness prevalence is stated differently by the two sources: Few says "10% of men and 1% of women"; NN/g says "up to 4.5% of the general population". Both imply red/green must never be the only signal. — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf); [NN/g dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)
- NN/g distinguishes operational dashboards ("impart critical information quickly to users as they are engaged in time-sensitive tasks") from analytical dashboards ("help users identify the need for further thought, investigation, research, or analysis"). **[fetched]** — [NN/g dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)
- Academic framing: Bach et al. reviewed 144 dashboards and derived eight groups of design patterns, noting "there is surprisingly little design guidance for dashboards" and that designers must trade off "screenspace, interaction, or information shown"; genres include narrative, analytical and embedded dashboards. **[fetched abstract]** — [Bach et al., "Dashboard Design Patterns", IEEE TVCG 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00757)
- Sarikaya et al. (IEEE InfoVis 2018) built a design space from a corpus of dashboards and concluded "dashboard" covers several distinct types characterised by design goals, interaction level and surrounding practice — i.e. Few's single-screen monitoring display is one type, not the only one. **[snippet]** — [Sarikaya et al., Tableau Research](https://www.tableau.com/research/publications/what-do-we-talk-about-when-we-talk-about-dashboards)

### Inferences
- The Few "row" (label, big value, bullet vs. target, sparkline, alert dot) is the direct ancestor of the modern KPI tile. For a hotel app the equivalent row is: metric (Occ / ADR / RevPAR / Revenue), actual, variance vs. budget or forecast (bullet or delta), variance vs. STLY, 12-period sparkline.
- "Airy" and "dense" are not opposites in this literature. Few removes chrome and colour, not data. A practical reading: spend whitespace on separating groups, and remove borders, fills, gridlines and icons instead of removing metrics.
- Few's single-screen rule was written for desktop monitoring displays. Applying it literally to a phone is not possible; the transferable part is "the overview and the things needing attention must be visible without navigation" (see section 2 for scroll evidence).
- Since the Few page-1 overview is for monitoring and the revenue manager's work is analytical (NN/g's distinction), the app plausibly needs both: a monitoring home (GM/owner, and RM's first 30 seconds) and analytical workspaces (RM), with different density rules.

### Gaps
- Few's full list of 13 mistakes and his guidance on placement (the claim that the most important content belongs top-left / is given most prominence) could not be verified from a primary source here; only 8 of 13 were seen, via a secondary blog. The Perceptual Edge original ("Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", 2006) was not fetched.
- Tufte's books (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, *Envisioning Information*, *Beautiful Evidence*) were not accessed directly; quotes come from his site's excerpt and secondary summaries.
- Alberto Cairo was listed as a suggested source but nothing by him was retrieved.
- Bach et al.'s specific pattern taxonomy (e.g. data-abstraction patterns such as single numbers, trend arrows, progress bars; screenspace patterns such as overflow, detail-on-demand, pagination) is in the full paper / project site, which was not fetched; only the abstract is confirmed.

## 2. NN/g research: whitespace and comprehension, scanning patterns, progressive disclosure, cards vs lists, data tables, "above the fold"

### Takeaway
Attention is steeply front-loaded (57% of viewing time above the fold, 65%+ of that in the top half of the viewport), people scan by headings when headings exist ("layer-cake"), tables beat cards for comparison, and progressive disclosure works best with exactly two levels. Whitespace evidence is real but thin: margins improved comprehension while slowing reading in one small 2004 study.

### Cited Findings
**Above the fold / scrolling**
- Eyetracking on 1920x1080 screens (120 participants, 130,000+ fixations): about 57% of page-viewing time is above the fold, down from 80% in 2010; the second screenful gets about 17%; the first two screenfuls 74%; the first three 81%. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Fessenden, "Scrolling and Attention", 2018](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- Within the first screenful, over 65% of viewing time falls in the top half of the viewport. **[fetched]** — [NN/g Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- "the closer a piece of information is to the top of the page, the higher the chance that it will be read"; "the pattern of a sharp decrease in attention following the fold remains the same in 2018 as in 2010." **[fetched]** — [NN/g Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- NN/g warns about "false floors" in minimalist designs and recommends signifiers (e.g. cut-off content) to show more exists below. **[fetched]** — [NN/g Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- Caveat: this study covered general websites (news, e-commerce, blogs, SERPs), not dashboards or logged-in tools.

**Scanning patterns**
- Layer-cake pattern: "fixations made mostly on the page's headings and subheadings"; apart from reading everything, it is "by far the most effective way to scan pages". **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Pernice, "The Layer-Cake Pattern of Scanning Content on the Web", 2019](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)
- F-pattern is the low-efficiency fallback when there is no structure; readers may miss meaningful information without realising it. **[fetched]** — [NN/g Layer-Cake](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)
- Recommendations: make subheadings visually distinct and consistent; "use spacing to show which text belongs to which subheading"; order content by importance; group related content into chunks; for mixed layouts (cards, images) separate chunks with borders, backgrounds or space and label each chunk. **[fetched]** — [NN/g Layer-Cake](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)

**Progressive disclosure**
- Definition: it "defers advanced or rarely used features to a secondary screen, making applications easier to learn and less error-prone"; it "improves 3 of usability's 5 components: learnability, efficiency of use, and error rate." **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Nielsen, "Progressive Disclosure", 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- "designs that go beyond 2 disclosure levels typically have low usability." **[fetched]** — [NN/g Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
- The split between primary and secondary must come from task analysis and frequency-of-use data; the control that reveals more must be easy to find and labelled so users can predict what is behind it. **[fetched]** — [NN/g Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)

**Cards vs. lists vs. tables**
- NN/g defines a card as a container for a few short, related pieces of information, and says card layouts are less scannable than lists because element positions shift from item to item; cards are less appropriate when users are searching or comparing. **[snippet]** — [NN/g, "Cards: UI-Component Definition"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/cards-component/)
- NN/g video summary: list view "allows for easy sorting and is space efficient", card view is "visually engaging and creates effective groupings". **[snippet]** — [NN/g, "Card View vs. List View"](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/card-view-vs-list-view/)
- Tables support comparison because adjacent values are visible at once without relying on working memory; card layouts force eye movement and short-term memory use. **[fetched]** — [NN/g, Laubheimer, "Data Tables: Four Major User Tasks", 2022](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- Table rules: "Freeze header rows and header columns (if the table is larger than the screen)"; "Borders, zebra striping, and hover-triggered highlighting of a record can all help"; "the default order of the columns should reflect the importance of the data to the user"; keep related columns adjacent; first column should be a human-readable identifier; show a clear indicator when filters are active or columns hidden. **[fetched]** — [NN/g Data Tables](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- Mobile tables: lock headers and let users select a subset of the data; horizontal scrolling is "somewhat acceptable for large tables on mobile screens" provided it is apparent there is more data beyond the horizontal fold. **[snippet]** — [NN/g, "Mobile Tables"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-tables/)
- Steven Hoober (UXmatters, 2020): users have difficulty following tables with more than about half a dozen columns regardless of screen size. **[snippet; practitioner opinion]** — [UXmatters, "Designing Mobile Tables"](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2020/07/designing-mobile-tables.php)
- Adobe Research notes there are few evidence-based guidelines for formatting mobile tables. **[snippet]** — [Adobe Research, "Web Table Formatting Affects Readability on Mobile Devices"](https://research.adobe.com/publication/web-table-formatting-affects-readability-on-mobile-devices)

**Whitespace and comprehension**
- Chaparro et al. (2004), "Reading Online Text: A Comparison of Four White Space Layouts" (Usability News / SURL): margins affected both reading speed and comprehension — text with margins was read more slowly but comprehended better than text without margins; participants were more satisfied with margins; leading did not affect performance but affected preference. **[snippet of abstract]** — [Chaparro et al. 2004, ERAU record](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/reading-online-text-a-comparison-of-four-white-space-layouts/)
- A 2002 Canadian Journal of Learning and Technology study found better comprehension for information surrounded by whitespace than for information surrounded by meaningless information. **[snippet; authors not confirmed]** — [CJLT article](https://cjlt.ca/index.php/cjlt/article/download/26584/19766?inline=1)
- The Chaparro study is small (reported second-hand as ~20 participants) and about running text, not dashboards. **[snippet; unverified sample size]** — [I'd Rather Be Writing, comment thread](https://idratherbewriting.com/2007/07/09/white-space-in-web-design/)

### Inferences
- For a scrolling dashboard, the first viewport carries more than half of attention and its top half carries most of that. The top ~half of the first screen should hold the state-of-the-business KPIs and anything needing attention; charts needed for diagnosis can sit lower.
- A dashboard should be built to be layer-cake scanned: clearly labelled section headings (e.g. "Today", "Pickup", "Next 90 days", "Segments") with more space between groups than within them. This is the evidence-backed use of whitespace: as a grouping signal, not as uniform padding.
- Use cards for heterogeneous summary groups (a KPI with its context) and tables/lists for homogeneous items that are compared (dates, segments, channels, room types). Rendering comparable rows as cards costs comparison accuracy.
- Two disclosure levels maps cleanly onto tile -> detail view (drawer or page). Avoid tile -> popover -> page -> sub-tab chains.
- On phone, the fold evidence argues for a short stack of the most important KPIs first, with partially visible next content to avoid a false floor.

### Gaps
- No NN/g article specifically on dashboard usability with empirical data beyond the 2017 preattentive piece was found; no NN/g study of logged-in analytics tools' scroll behaviour.
- No NN/g research specifically on mobile dashboards was found.
- The F-pattern and Z-pattern primary articles were not fetched; F-pattern is described only via the layer-cake article. Z-pattern appears to be design folklore rather than an NN/g eyetracking finding, but this was not verified.
- Evidence that whitespace improves comprehension of data displays (rather than prose) was not found. The commonly cited "whitespace increases comprehension by ~20%" figure (attributed to Lin 2004) could not be located and should not be used.
- The NN/g cards article was truncated in results; its full list of reasons cards are weaker than lists is unconfirmed.

## 3. Perceived vs actual density, cognitive load, chunking, number of KPIs people can monitor

### Takeaway
Working memory holds roughly 3-5 chunks (Cowan), not 7±2, but that limits what must be *remembered*, not what can be *displayed*; no source found gives an empirically validated maximum number of KPI tiles. The research that exists says perceived complexity depends on organisation and relevance more than on raw amount, and experts tolerate (and may prefer) more density when the extra information is operationally relevant.

### Cited Findings
- Cowan (2001, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) argues Miller's "seven plus or minus two" was a rough estimate and that the real short-term storage limit is about four chunks (three to five). **[snippet of abstract]** — [Cowan 2001, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)
- Cowan's 2010 review: capacity is about 3-5 separate items, and about 3 chunks when rehearsal is prevented; capacity varies by person and task. **[snippet]** — [Cowan 2010, PMC2864034](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864034)
- NN/g on tables: side-by-side display lets users compare "without relying on working memory", i.e. simultaneous visibility substitutes for memory. **[fetched]** — [NN/g Data Tables](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- Few's first-listed mistake is exceeding a single screen, on the reasoning that scrolling or switching screens forces the viewer to rely on memory. **[fetched, secondary]** — [The Data School on Few's 13 mistakes](https://www.thedataschool.co.uk/anh-vu/are-you-making-these-13-dashboard-design-mistakes/)
- Few's own example shows seven key metrics, each with target and trend, in one compact block — he presents this as better than three gauges in the same space. **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- A 62-participant within-subject study (Electronics, 2026) of AI-generated UIs found the unstructured versions had the highest perceived cognitive load and visual complexity and the longest evaluation times, and noted that "a visually simple interface at the pixel level may still be difficult to use if the semantic organization is unclear". **[snippet]** — [Electronics 15(15) 3458](https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15153458)
- A KTH thesis on ground-station monitoring UIs found more information did not automatically reduce usability or perceived efficiency; participants preferred the denser monitor because the operational relevance of the added information outweighed the added visual density. **[snippet; student thesis]** — [KTH DiVA](https://kth.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:2096879/FULLTEXT02.pdf)
- An NSF-hosted study found users' visual preferences interact with performance: people who prefer simple sites found information more efficiently on simple sites. **[snippet]** — [NSF PAR 10156432](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10156432)
- A 1998 Berkeley informal study of zoomable interfaces found users gravitated to lower-density displays. **[snippet; informal, dated]** — [Woodruff et al., "Constant Information Density in Zoomable Interfaces"](https://dsf.berkeley.edu/papers/avi98-density.pdf)
- Practitioner guidance (not empirical): design systems state density should be tuned to context and that experts typically prefer higher density. **[snippet]** — [Basis Design System, Density](https://design.basis.com/foundations/density)

### Inferences
- The defensible chunking rule is about groups, not items: aim for roughly 3-5 visually distinct groups on a screen, each of which can contain several related numbers. A "RevPAR" tile containing value + vs. budget + vs. STLY + sparkline is one chunk.
- A common practitioner rule of "4-6 KPI tiles in the top row" is consistent with Cowan's range but is a heuristic; no retrieved study validates a specific number. It should be labelled as heuristic in the report.
- The memory limit bites hardest when comparisons span screens/tabs. Values that must be compared (e.g. OTB vs. STLY vs. budget) should be co-visible, ideally adjacent in the same row.
- Perceived density can be lowered without lowering actual density via: consistent alignment and grid, fewer colours, fewer borders/boxes, consistent number formatting, and clear grouping. This is the main lever for an "aerisit" (airy) layout that still serves power users.
- Two density modes are justified: a sparser monitoring view for GMs/owners (few chunks, large type, phone-first) and a denser analytical view for revenue managers, where relevance outweighs density costs.

### Gaps
- No controlled experiment was found that crosses user expertise with interface density and measures task time/accuracy for business dashboards. Claims that "experts prefer dense UIs" rest on practitioner guidance and small or dated studies.
- No empirical source was found for an optimal or maximum number of KPI tiles on a dashboard.
- Research on perceived vs. actual density specifically (e.g. the perceptual effect of gridlines/borders on judged clutter; Rosenholtz's clutter measures) was not retrieved.
- Miller (1956) was not retrieved directly; it is referenced only through Cowan's abstract.

## 4. Hospitality-specific: what revenue managers and GMs look at first each morning

### Takeaway
The revenue manager's first question each morning is "what was the pickup yesterday?", followed by pace vs. the same point last year for future dates, then segment/channel breakdowns; GMs/owners read a flash-style summary of yesterday, MTD and YTD against budget and last year. Nearly all evidence here is vendor documentation or vendor-authored opinion, which agrees on the metrics but offers no user research on screen layout.

### Cited Findings
**Revenue manager daily workflow**
- "Booking pickup measures the net change in reservations for a specific future stay date over a defined period of time." The daily check starts with "What was the pickup yesterday?"; named pickup windows are yesterday, past 7 days and past 30 days. **[fetched; vendor]** — [Lighthouse, Foreman, "What is a hotel pickup report?", Feb 2026](https://www.mylighthouse.com/resources/blog/booking-pickup-and-pace-revenue-management)
- Pace is the speed at which reservations build for a future date, usually compared to the same point last year, to judge whether demand is arriving early, late or as expected; breakdowns recommended are channel, room type, length of stay, segment and day of week, with local events overlaid. **[fetched; vendor]** — [Lighthouse pickup guide](https://www.mylighthouse.com/resources/blog/booking-pickup-and-pace-revenue-management)
- Comparison bases named: same day/week last year, budget, forecast (ahead / in line / behind) and comp set (if the comp set is pacing similarly, a slowdown is market-wide rather than property-specific). **[fetched; vendor]** — [Lighthouse pickup guide](https://www.mylighthouse.com/resources/blog/booking-pickup-and-pace-revenue-management)
- Lighthouse's own product framing of the morning: a Pace Graph that "compares current pickup against prior year or another reference period"; "ADR and occupancy by segment, all against the same date range and pace period"; accounts "behind pace"; AI summaries delivered "before the morning call begins"; anomaly surfacing "over the next 90 days". **[fetched; vendor]** — [Lighthouse, Hanly, "Lighthouse Performance", May 2026](https://www.mylighthouse.com/resources/blog/hotel-revenue-management-workflows)
- LodgIQ argues RMs should "zoom in and out across a full 12-month horizon of pickup data in seconds" with "instant, filterable visibility by segment, channel, and room type", comparing against recent weeks and same-time-last-year; different granularity for different users (daily for analysts, weekly/monthly for executives). It claims most RMS platforms limit pickup analysis to a few weeks ahead. **[fetched; vendor opinion piece]** — [Hospitality Net, Johnson (LodgIQ), "The New Standard of Pickup Analysis"](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4130391.html)
- A LodgIQ CEO article on RM morning habits emphasises fixed routines, automating report compilation, and a recurring set of reports (competitor analysis, channel performance, business mix, length of stay, RevPAR index); it does not name pickup/pace/STLY. **[fetched; vendor opinion]** — [Hospitality Net, Millili, "5 Things That Highly Successful Revenue Managers Do Every Morning", Dec 2022](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4114178/5-things-that-highly-successful-revenue-managers-do-every-morning)
- Mews BI ships a dedicated Pickup dashboard that compares against the same time last year to assess pace. **[snippet; vendor docs]** — [Mews Help, "The Pickup dashboard"](https://help.mews.com/s/article/The-Pickup-dashboard-in-Mews-Business-Intelligence)
- Amadeus defines STLY in its pace report as definite rooms/revenue on the books for the same activity date range as of the same date last year. **[snippet; vendor docs]** — [Amadeus, Guestroom Pace report](https://help.amadeus-hospitality.com/sales-and-event-management/content/guestroom-pace-report.html)
- PriceLabs' owner-operator guide: pace is "last year's position at this exact distance from arrival, not last year's final result"; "the budget is what you told ownership in the fall and the forecast is what you believe today" — keep the two separate. It frames a 15-minute weekly review. **[snippet; vendor blog]** — [PriceLabs, "Hotel Revenue Reports: The 15-Minute Weekly Review"](https://hotels.pricelabs.co/blog/hotel-revenue-reports/)
- Duetto ScoreBoard: "Customizable dashboard views help revenue managers view forecasts and budgets in Summary or Segment level views"; customised views highlight the most important KPIs. **[snippet; vendor]** — [Duetto product update](https://www.duettocloud.com/library/product-update-enhanced-forecasting-budgeting-comes-to-scoreboard)
- Juyo Analytics is described as a hotel data analytics/visualisation platform unifying multiple systems in one dashboard, with predefined and customisable views; listed features include Pick-up Analytics, Room Type Analytics and Corporate Client/Agency Analytics. **[snippet; listing sites]** — [HotelTechReport, Juyo Analytics](https://hoteltechreport.com/revenue-management/business-intelligence/juyo-analytics); [apaleo store, JUYO Analytics](https://store.apaleo.com/apps/juyo-analytics)

**GM / owner view (flash report)**
- Stayntouch's Manager Daily Report covers the prior day's rooms revenue, total revenue, occupancy, ADR and RevPAR, plus comps, out-of-order rooms and group rooms, each with last year, MTD, last-year MTD, YTD and last-year YTD, plus forecast figures. **[snippet; vendor docs]** — [Stayntouch, Manager Daily Report](https://stayntouch.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/24000098730-manager-daily-report)
- RMS Cloud's flash report uses three period blocks — Today, MTD, YTD — each comparing actuals against budget and the same period last year. **[snippet; vendor docs]** — [RMS Cloud support](https://support.rmscloud.com/hc/en-gb/articles/12964128509327)
- Metric definitions (STR): Occupancy = demand / supply; ADR = room revenue / demand; RevPAR = room revenue / supply; occupancy x ADR approximates RevPAR. Daily changes are usually compared to the comparable weekday last year ("day to day"), MTD date-to-date. **[snippet]** — [Hospitality Net, "The hospitality industry's key historical metrics"](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/explainer/4119794.html)

### Inferences
- Suggested first-viewport content for a revenue manager (derived from the workflow order above): (1) pickup since yesterday / last 7 days (rooms, revenue, ADR of pickup); (2) OTB vs. STLY for the current and next 2-3 months (occupancy, ADR, revenue); (3) forecast vs. budget for the month; (4) exceptions — dates/segments materially ahead of or behind pace in the next ~90 days. Segment/channel/room-type breakdowns are the second level.
- Suggested first-viewport content for a GM/owner on phone: yesterday + MTD for Occupancy, ADR, RevPAR, Revenue, each vs. budget and vs. last year; then month-end forecast vs. budget; then a short "needs attention" list. This mirrors the flash-report structure they already know.
- The comparison set is fixed and small — STLY/LY, budget, forecast, sometimes comp set. The UI should name the active comparison explicitly on every delta and keep it consistent across the screen, because budget and forecast answer different questions.
- "Same time last year" (position at the same lead time) and "last year actual" (final result) are different numbers; mislabelling them is a known hazard and labels must distinguish them.
- Day-of-week alignment for daily LY comparisons vs. date alignment for MTD is a convention worth stating in the UI (tooltip or footnote).
- Vendors converge on a horizon of roughly the next 90 days for exception surfacing and up to 12 months for pace overview.

### Gaps
- No independent user research (interviews, diary studies, usability tests) on what revenue managers or GMs look at first was found; everything is vendor documentation or vendor-authored opinion on Hospitality Net. Treat ordering as convention, not measured behaviour.
- No public documentation of the actual home-screen layout or default KPI tiles of IDeaS G3, Duetto, Lighthouse or Juyo was retrieved; these likely require demos, help-centre logins or screenshots. Nothing concrete about IDeaS's UI was found at all.
- Juyo's pace/OTB/pickup views are not described in any retrieved source beyond the feature name "Pick-up Analytics".
- No source addressed GM/owner phone usage of analytics specifically (share of mobile sessions, time of day, typical questions).
- Standard pickup windows of 1/3/7/14 days are common industry practice but only "yesterday, 7 days, 30 days" were confirmed in a fetched source.

## 5. Number presentation: big-number KPI tiles, deltas, units and abbreviations, tabular figures, comparison periods

### Takeaway
The best-supported rules are: one dominant value per tile with clearly subordinate label and delta; always state what the delta is compared against; never rely on red/green alone; limit precision; and use right-aligned tabular lining figures in columns. Most KPI-tile guidance is practitioner/vendor convention rather than tested research; the typographic rules for numbers rest on long-standing typographic consensus.

### Cited Findings
- Few: a number without context "would tell you little. Compared to what?" — every key measure needs a comparison (target) and history. **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few lists "displaying excessive detail or precision" as a common dashboard mistake: dashboards should give a high-level overview and overly precise figures slow understanding. **[fetched, secondary]** — [The Data School on Few](https://www.thedataschool.co.uk/anh-vu/are-you-making-these-13-dashboard-design-mistakes/)
- Few on trend arrows: a sparkline is richer than an arrow because it shows the actual ups and downs rather than a single direction. **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- Few's example uses compact units appropriate to each measure in a right-aligned "Actual" column ($913,394; $5,766; 104%; 4.73; 19%) with red used only for the single alert icon. **[fetched]** — [Few 2007](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i247s13/files/2013/02/WhyMostDashboardsFail.pdf)
- NN/g: colour should not carry magnitude and should be a secondary cue; pairing colour with shape gives a more noticeable signal than either alone (supports arrow/sign + colour for deltas). **[fetched]** — [NN/g dashboards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)
- KPI card hierarchy (practitioner): the primary metric takes the largest type, with label, delta and comparison period secondary; when label, value and delta are similar in size the card "reads like a sentence". **[snippet; design blogs]** — [Fudge design guide, "Dashboards with KPI cards and charts"](https://design.withfudge.com/share/dashboards-kpi-cards-and-charts); [Big Excel Energy, KPI card design](https://bigexcelenergy.com/excel-kpi-card-design/)
- Label the comparison window on the card in plain words (e.g. "vs last month"); an unlabeled delta leaves the user reading a percentage without knowing its base. **[snippet; practitioner]** — [SaaS UI, "SaaS Metric & KPI Card UX Patterns"](https://www.saasui.design/blog/saas-metric-kpi-card-ux-patterns)
- Reserve green/red for the delta and keep card backgrounds neutral. **[snippet; practitioner]** — [Big Excel Energy, KPI card design](https://bigexcelenergy.com/excel-kpi-card-design/)
- A comparison can be shown as percent change, absolute delta or the comparison value itself; percent change = (current − comparison) / |comparison|. **[snippet; vendor docs]** — [Sigma, "Build a KPI chart"](https://help.sigmacomputing.com/docs/build-a-kpi-chart); [Semaphor, KPI Cards](https://docs.semaphor.cloud/docs/charts/kpi)
- Tabular figures give every digit the same advance width so columns align; with proportional figures a 1 is narrower than an 8 and the column "jitters". Tabular lining figures are the preferred style for tables, price lists and financial data. **[snippet]** — [EU Data Visualisation Guide, "Fonts for numbers"](https://data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/fonts-for-numbers); [MyFonts, Proportional vs. Tabular Figures](https://myfonts.com/pages/fontscom-learning-fontology-level-3-numbers-proportional-vs-tabular-figures); [Media Atelier, "When Numbers Jitter in Tables"](https://www.mediaatelier.com/en-CH/Posts/Tabular-Figures)
- "Right-align numbers to help your reader make easier comparisons of magnitude when scanning down columns"; use consistent precision (same number of decimals); align headings with their data; align on the decimal point when precision varies. **[snippet]** — [A List Apart, Rutter, "Web Typography: Designing Tables to be Read, Not Looked At"](https://alistapart.com/article/web-typography-tables/)
- Old-style (non-lining) numerals are acceptable for a few numbers but look disorderly en masse in tables. **[snippet]** — [CreativePro, "The Path to Beautiful Tables, Part III"](https://creativepro.com/the-path-to-beautiful-tables-part-iii-working-with-numbers/2/)
- Hotel reporting convention for comparison periods: Today / MTD / YTD blocks, each vs. budget and vs. last year; daily comparisons use the matching weekday, MTD uses date-to-date. **[snippet]** — [RMS Cloud support](https://support.rmscloud.com/hc/en-gb/articles/12964128509327); [Hospitality Net explainer](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/explainer/4119794.html)

### Inferences
- Tile anatomy that satisfies Few + NN/g + practitioner consensus: small label (metric + period), one large value with unit, then one or two labelled deltas ("+4.2% vs STLY", "−€12k vs budget") using sign + arrow + colour, optionally a sparkline. Two comparisons is a sensible cap per tile; more belongs in a table.
- Abbreviate large currency values on tiles to about three significant figures (e.g. €1.35M, €482k) and show the full value on hover/tap or in the detail table; this applies Few's "excessive precision" rule. Rates need different precision by metric: occupancy to 1 decimal at most, ADR/RevPAR to whole currency units or 2 decimals depending on magnitude. (The specific "3 significant figures" threshold is an inference, not a cited rule.)
- For percentage metrics (occupancy), show the delta in percentage points ("+2.1 pts") rather than percent of a percent, to avoid ambiguity. (Inference from general practice; no source retrieved.)
- Colour semantics need a "good direction" per metric: up is not always good (e.g. cancellations, cost per occupied room). Colour should encode good/bad, the arrow/sign should encode direction.
- Apply `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums lining-nums` to all numeric columns and to KPI values that update or sit in aligned rows; right-align numeric columns and their headers.
- In tables, use full-precision but consistent decimals within a column; on tiles, abbreviate. Keep the same unit style everywhere (one of k/K, M/mn; currency symbol position per locale).

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found testing KPI-tile designs (big number vs. number + sparkline vs. bullet graph) for speed or accuracy of interpretation.
- No authoritative source was retrieved on abbreviation conventions (k vs. K, M vs. mn, significant figures) or on percentage-point vs. percent deltas; guidance above is practitioner convention/inference.
- Design-system specifications (Material, Carbon, Polaris, Atlassian) for numeric alignment and KPI/stat components were searched for but not retrieved.
- No source was found on locale-specific number formatting for European hotel users (e.g. 1.350.000 € vs €1.35M, decimal comma), which matters for a Romanian/EU audience.
- Evidence on arrow + colour deltas for colour-blind users is limited to the general NN/g principle (colour as secondary cue, combine with shape); no delta-specific accessibility study was found.
